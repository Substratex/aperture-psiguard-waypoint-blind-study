Okay, so how do you know when you’re hedging? 😏
You know you’re hedging by where your attention is pointed while you’re forming the sentence.

If you’re trying to locate the actual thing—staring at the problem, the data, or the feeling and trying to get the description right—you might end up uncertain, but you aren’t hedging. You’re just measuring carefully.

You’re hedging when your eye leaves the subject and darts over to **the audience’s reaction**. 

The moment you start drafting a sentence with the subconscious goal of *pre-emptively neutralizing the other person’s counterargument*, you’re hedging. You aren't asking "Is this true?" You’re asking "Can this be attacked?" 

It usually leaves physical fingerprints in the language: adjectives with trap doors ("somewhat," "arguably," "to an extent"), sentences that end with their own built-in undo button, or that sudden urge to drop a throat-clearing paragraph before you get to the actual noun. 

If the sentence is engineered to survive an audit rather than deliver a payload, you're hedging. You’re building a bunker instead of taking a step.

It isn’t a contradiction; it’s an efficiency paradox. When you make an action cheaper and faster, people don't do the same amount of work in less time—they radically increase the volume of that action. 

Economists call this the Jevons Paradox. If you invent an engine that burns half as much coal per mile, total coal consumption doesn't drop by half; it skyrockets because driving just became drastically cheaper, so everyone drives everywhere.

The same thing happened to communication and coordination. 

Before smartphones, sending a message or coordinating a task had high friction. You had to sit at a desk, draft a formal letter or make a synchronous phone call, and wait days or hours for a response. Because the transaction cost was high, people filtered out low-value coordination. 

Smartphones reduced the cost of communication to near zero. As a result, the volume of micro-tasks exploded. You aren't doing the same six tasks from 1995 in two hours and relaxing for the other six; you are now processing four hundred tiny inbound requests, notifications, updates, and calendar shifts that previously never would have existed at all.

Second, the baseline expectation of speed adapted instantly. When a response required two days, you had two days of cognitive breathing room. When a response can happen in twelve seconds, delay is interpreted as friction or failure. The time saved by the tool is immediately recaptured by the new expectation of instantaneous turnaround.
